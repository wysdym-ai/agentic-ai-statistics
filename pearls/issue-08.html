📰 ABOVE THE FOLD · the week's news, with teeth

Everybody is more productive. Almost nobody is more profitable. Computerworld rounded up the latest ROI numbers and they do not flatter anyone. In McKinsey's survey about 80% said AI improved productivity, 37% said it showed up in profits, and only 6% said it accounted for at least 5% of operating profit. Gartner puts the 2025 odds of an AI initiative achieving ROI at one in five, and says three in five IT leaders worry about agents running up unexpected costs. (Computerworld, Oct 5)
Productivity is what you report when you cannot find the profit.

The man with the nine-billion-dollar marketing budget says nobody knows the ROI. Booking Holdings spends roughly $8 billion to $9 billion a year on paid customer-acquisition channels, and about a third of its traffic still arrives that way. Its CFO told Fortune that customers using Booking's AI tools book somewhat faster, convert at somewhat higher rates and cancel less often. Then he said the quiet part about the model builders: "they don't really know what is going to be the ROI." (Fortune, Oct 5)
"Somewhat faster, somewhat higher" is the most honest AI results slide of the year. Frame it.

You pay for intelligence twice. OpenAI and Anthropic are now competing on who keeps less of your data, and Fortune reports more companies are drifting toward open models and "sovereign AI" because of it. The line worth keeping is Satya Nadella's: "You essentially pay for intelligence twice, once with money, and again with something even more valuable: the proprietary knowledge you must reveal to make that intelligence useful." (Fortune, Oct 5)
Your call transcripts, pricing and loss reasons are the second payment. Worth knowing which of your GTM tools is collecting it.

Twenty AI agents now live in your text messages. TechCrunch counted 20 assistants you reach by texting them like a person, no app required. The money behind them is not small: Instinct raised $1 billion in September at a $10 billion valuation, and Town raised a $55 million Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz and Forerunner. Plans start around $9.99 a month. (TechCrunch, Oct 3)
Your buyer's assistant is moving into the one channel your SDRs already got blocked on.

🦪 THE PEARL · the one earnest bit
A pilot has to be interesting. A line item has to be defended.
For two years, AI in GTM lived in the pilot budget. Nobody asks a pilot to pay for itself. It only has to be interesting.

That has changed. The agents now sit on a budget line, and the line has a name next to it. Possibly yours.

A line item gets a different question at renewal. Not "is it working?" but "what did it return?" Most teams cannot answer, and not because the agents failed. Nobody built the accounting: which agent touched which deal, what it changed, and whether the deal moved.

Activity is easy to count. Emails drafted, records updated, hours saved. None of it is an answer.

The accounting has to exist before the review, not after it. Start small. Tie one agent to one deal stage, and measure whether deals leave that stage faster.

🛸 THE WEIRD WIRE · two AI stories too strange to make up

Google closed a bug bounty because the bots would not stop filing. Google paused its open source Vulnerability Reward Program on October 1, with an update promised for the first quarter of 2027. Its explanation is a sentence every inbound team will recognise: "This pause is due to a significant rise in automated submissions, the vast majority of which are not valid." Per Tom's Hardware, engineers and maintainers were buried in reports that were invalid or simply hallucinated. (TechCrunch, Oct 4)
A program that paid people to find problems, closed by machines confidently reporting problems that do not exist. If that sounds like your lead queue, it should.

The most honest byline in marketing belongs to a bot. MarTech runs articles by "MarTechBot," and its author bio is a small masterpiece of disclosure: "I am BETA software powered by AI. I will make mistakes, errors and sometimes even invent things, but all of my articles are reviewed by human editors before they're published." This week it wrote about how to maximize ROI. (MarTech, Oct 5)
Full marks. That is more disclosure than any AI SDR email has ever managed, and it has an editor.

🧰 THE STACK · stuff we're having fun playing with

1. Google Postmaster Tools. The one we should have set up eight issues ago. Add your sending domain, verify it with a DNS record, and Gmail shows you your spam rate, domain reputation, authentication results and delivery errors for mail sent to personal Gmail accounts. It costs nothing and takes ten minutes. If your team sends sequences, newsletters or anything an agent drafts, this is the chart that tells you whether any of it arrives. (Gmail Help)

2. Salesforce Hosted MCP Servers. The detail that matters is per-user sign-in: the agent acts with that user's existing permissions, field-level security and sharing rules, instead of through a service account that can see everything. You can expose Flows and Apex actions as tools without writing connector code. It also gives you a clean answer to this week's Pearl, because every action lands under a named user. (Salesforce Developers)

The prompt. Rehearse the renewal conversation before somebody schedules it. Paste this into the model of your choice along with whatever numbers you have:

You are a skeptical CFO reviewing our AI agent spend for next
year. I am the GTM leader who owns the line item.

I will paste what we spend and what I think it returned.

1. Sort every claim I make into one of three piles:
   activity (things the agents did), efficiency (time saved),
   or outcome (deals, revenue or cost that changed).
2. For each activity or efficiency claim, ask the one question
   that would turn it into an outcome, or kill it.
3. Tell me which agent you would cut first, and why.
4. Tell me the single number you would want on one page
   before approving the same budget again.

Do not be polite. If I am counting hours saved as money,
say so.

The share of displaced workers with a direct path into a growing job, according to a new McKinsey Global Institute report covered by Fortune. Its base case has AI and automation cutting demand for about 36 million US jobs by 2035 while growth elsewhere creates about 41 million. (Fortune, Oct 3)
The jobs exist. The bridge to them mostly does not. Your enablement budget just became labour-market policy.
