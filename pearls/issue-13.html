📰 ABOVE THE FOLD · the week's news, with teeth

Outlook.com bounced real email for days. Microsoft says it was a bug. From late last week, Outlook.com rejected legitimate messages with vague SPF and DMARC errors, some of them one-line notes from small senders. Microsoft first called it a "service degradation," then confirmed in incident EX1545193 that "a recent update to the spam enforcement rules caused email messages to be incorrectly marked and blocked." Senders who matched Microsoft's SPF, DKIM and DMARC spec say mail is flowing again. (The Register, Nov 9)
Your sequence kept running the whole time. It is very good at following up on emails nobody received.

Forrester: the AI bubble isn't bursting yet. Plan your exit anyway. About nine-tenths of the money building AI infrastructure comes from large, healthy tech companies paying cash, Forrester says, so no crash is imminent. But the build-out only breaks even if AI lifts US GDP 5% by 2035, and productivity covers about 2 points of that. Its advice: stress-test your AI plans against a key vendor ending support in 12 months, and know in advance which use cases you would drop. (The Register, Nov 9)
Step one of Forrester's plan is listing the AI tools you would cut. Step zero is listing the ones you have.

Microsoft switches Claude on for UK Copilot tenants on December 9. Unless an admin says no. Tenants that haven't picked a setting get Anthropic's models by default, with the data processed in the US. Doing nothing counts as yes. Claude is already on by default across most of Microsoft's commercial cloud. The EU, EFTA and UK were the exceptions, and the UK is coming off that list. (The Register, Nov 10)
The most powerful setting in enterprise AI is the one nobody opened. Check yours before December 9.

Meta's newest model is built for the boring 90%. Meta released Muse Ember, its lightest model yet, made for "the work agents repeat all day: tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing." It costs 10 cents per million input tokens, and Meta pitches it as the junior partner to its frontier model: "Muse Wonder plans, Muse Ember handles the volume." Last week Gartner said 9 in 10 agent steps are routine. Seven days later, Meta shipped a model for them. (TechCrunch, Nov 9)
A week ago the cheap model was a Gartner prediction. Now it has a brand name and a tagline.

🦪 THE PEARL · the one earnest bit
Make your agents earn the renewal.
Somewhere in finance is a renewal notice for an AI agent nobody has opened since August. It auto-renews in 30 days. Nobody will stop it, because nobody can say what it did.

Buying agents was easy. Keeping them is the real decision.

Before any renewal, check five things.

• Whether reps used it in a normal week. Count from week 8, because launch week lies.
• Whether it only did what it was allowed to do, and whether you can prove it.
• What happened to its output: approved, edited, overridden or ignored.
• Which deals it touched, and where those deals went next.
• What leaves with it if you cancel.

If you cannot answer three of the five, that gap is the finding.

Keep the agents that can show their work.

🛸 THE WEIRD WIRE · two AI stories too strange to make up

Clicking "don't ask again" leaked passwords to GitHub. When a Claude Code user approves a shell command with "Yes, and don't ask again," the command gets saved to a local settings file, sometimes with the password or API key still inside it. Security firm Apiiro found more than 100,000 of those files in public GitHub repos, and about 1,100, from 63 organizations, held real secrets. It was first reported in August 2025. The bug reports were closed after 60 days of inactivity. (Ars Technica, Nov 9)
Somebody said "don't ask again." The computer took them literally.

A Welsh radio station plays AI songs next to human ones and won't say which is which. AI Slop FM, a one-man project, strips out artist names and cover art, then asks listeners to grade each track from S to E. The best make a weekly chart called the Slop 40. Early submissions include a run of rude protest songs about HR departments and an opera about a blocked toilet. The founder says the toilet opera "runs through my head about once a day, whether I like it or not." (The Register, Nov 9)
Somebody please run this on outbound. Strip the logos off 50 cold emails and ask buyers which ones a human wrote.

🧰 THE STACK · stuff we're having fun playing with

1. Google Admin Toolbox: Check MX. Free and official. Type in your sending domain and it checks your MX and SPF records, plus DKIM if you give it a selector. It takes two minutes, and it tells you whether the next bounce is Microsoft's fault or yours. (Google Admin Toolbox)

2. gitleaks. Open-source scanner that finds passwords, API keys and tokens in git repos, including old commits. Run it on any repo your agents or your GTM engineers have touched before it goes public. Fair warning: its maintainer has marked it feature complete and now ships security patches only, so it does one job and stays that way. (GitHub)

3. DMARC.org overview. The plainest explainer we know of for SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Send it to whoever owns your domain, or read it yourself and find out that nobody does. (dmarc.org)

You are a RevOps analyst helping a GTM team plan for
an AI vendor going away.

I will paste the AI tools and agents we pay for, with
one line on what each one does.

For each tool:
1. Name the workflow that stops if it disappears
   tomorrow.
2. Say where its data and learned settings live, and
   whether we can export them.
3. Rate the switch EASY (a week), HARD (a quarter) or
   STUCK (we would rebuild the workflow).

Then give me:
- The tools we would drop first, and why.
- The one tool whose loss would hurt most.
- Three questions to ask that vendor before renewal.

If you do not know where a tool keeps its data, write
"unknown". Do not guess.
