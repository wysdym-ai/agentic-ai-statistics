Gartner says 9 in 10 steps your agents take are grunt work: classify, call a tool, pass the data along. An open-weight model does those for a quarter of the price of the cheapest closed model. Run a frontier model on every step and you pay up to 20 times more for the same filing.

The expensive model is doing the filing. You are paying it a partner's rate.

📰 ABOVE THE FOLD · the week's news, with teeth

Gartner: 90% of what your agents do needs no genius. Two Gartner analysts put it in writing last week. "Nine in 10 agent workflow steps are routine," and open-weight models handle them at parity for a quarter of the price of the cheapest closed model. Against a frontier model on every step the gap is as much as 20 to 1, or 5 or 6 to 1 once you count the open model's retries. Their planning assumption: by 2030, 90% of enterprise agent steps run on open weights, and the saving is the bonus. The real reason is control, since every routine step sent to a frontier API moves a slice of your workflow history outside your perimeter. (The Register, Nov 3)
The model-makers spent billions to be the brain. Gartner just told them the job is 90% filing.

Amazon wrote the memo every AI mandate needed, then sat on it for two months. A VP and Distinguished Engineer sent Amazon's engineers a treatise on working with AI in September; it went public this week. The useful parts: look at the teams burning the most tokens and check whether delivery rose to match, and "If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing." The backstory: in May Amazon shut down KiroRank, an employee-built leaderboard that ranked people by token usage, after staff spun up junk tasks to climb it. The practice now has a name, tokenmaxxing. (The Register, Oct 31)
Put a leaderboard on tokens and you get tokens. Put one on activities logged and you get, well, ask your reps.

Apple's CEO: "We don't want to ship AI for AI's sake." John Ternus, two months into the job, told Wired he likes where Apple is on AI after two years of "behind" headlines. His test for doing it right: people will not have to think about whether AI or regular software made the thing happen. Asked whether agents might make apps unnecessary, he said building products "is a deeply human and personal thing." (TechCrunch, Oct 31)
The biggest consumer company on earth declined to put "AI-powered" in the headline. Your pricing page has it four times.

Garry Tan spends up to $200,000 a year on tokens. The Y Combinator CEO told Walter Isaacson his agents cost him $100,000 to $200,000 a year. They have read all of his email, his Dropbox, his Apple Notes and 20 years of therapy notes. YC's internal agent, Stray Light, holds the full context of every founder it has backed and every application it has received. His own verdict: "It's too expensive right now," and "a crazy privilege that not everyone can do." (TechCrunch, Nov 2)
The most enthusiastic agent user in Silicon Valley says the bill is the problem. Gartner says 90% of it was avoidable. Read those two together before renewal.

🦪 THE PEARL · the one earnest bit
Cheaper tokens will not fix your agent bill.
Per-token prices fell more than 90% in two years. Agent bills went up anyway. Gartner calls it the inference paradox and expects inference cost per agentic workflow to rise fivefold by 2028.

The reason is plain. An agent plans, retrieves, reasons, calls a tool, checks, and tries again. Every step is a model call, and most carry the full context with them.

Now multiply by the number of agents that each read the same account from the same systems, separately, every day. You are not buying four units of intelligence. You are buying one unit of context four times.

Routing routine steps to a cheaper model helps, and you should do it. But that lowers the price of a call. The number of calls is what keeps rising, and that is set by how the stack is built.

Retrieve once, share everywhere. Measure cost per outcome, never per token.

🛸 THE WEIRD WIRE · two AI stories too strange to make up

Crooks are renting out your API keys for $60 a month. Google's Mandiant team says stolen LLM access, which it calls LLMjacking, now runs up charges above $100,000 a day at some victims and $1 million before anyone revokes the keys. One intruder made more than 500,000 API calls in under eight hours on stolen keys to power a service called Jet Night, which sells unlimited frontier-model tokens for about $60 a month against a going rate of $100 to $250. Jet Night keeps going down. The reason, per Google: victims find out and revoke the keys. (The Register, Nov 2)
There is a product with better unit economics than yours. It runs on your invoice.

Grok Bot users woke up on Halloween with their weekly limits already spent. Some went from 50% used to 100% overnight without touching it. Elon Musk confirmed on Sunday it was no glitch: "The cost of serving Grok Bot was unsustainably high relative to revenue." This is the product he called the fastest growing he has ever seen, inside a division that just reported a $431 million profit. He has not said what the limits will be from here. (TechCrunch, Nov 2)
The fastest-growing product in the company got throttled by the CEO on a Sunday. "Unsustainably high relative to revenue" is a sentence a lot of agent budgets will hear this quarter.

🧰 THE STACK · stuff we're having fun playing with

1. gstack. Garry Tan's open-source set of Claude Code skills, the ones behind the $200,000 token bill above. It turns a coding agent into a virtual team: a CEO role that pushes back on your framing, a reviewer, a QA lead that opens a real browser, a security officer, a release engineer. All Markdown, MIT licence, two pastes to install. Worth reading even if you never run it, because the role structure is a free lesson in how to brief an agent. (GitHub)

2. Ollama. The simplest way to run an open-weight model on your own laptop or server. If the Gartner note made you want to test the "90% routine" claim, this is the afternoon experiment: pull a model, point one classification or data-passing step at it, compare the output with what the frontier model gives you for 4 to 20 times the price. The docs cover tool calling, structured outputs and embeddings. (docs.ollama.com)

3. Hugging Face Inference Providers. One API and one key across many hosts for open-weight models, so you can run the same step on three different models without three contracts. Pairs with the prompt below. (Hugging Face docs)

The prompt. Before you argue about which model, find out which steps. Paste one agent workflow in with this:

You are an AI cost analyst for a go-to-market team.

I will paste the steps of one agent workflow, in order
(for example: pull CRM record, summarize last three calls,
classify intent, draft email, check against brand rules,
write back to CRM).

For each step:
1. Label it ROUTINE (classify, extract, pass, validate,
   reconcile, format) or REASONING (judgment, tradeoffs,
   ambiguous input, customer-facing wording).
2. Say in one sentence what breaks if a smaller model
   gets it wrong.
3. Say whether a human should see the output before the
   next step runs.

Then give me:
- The count of ROUTINE vs REASONING steps.
- The three steps to move to a cheaper model first.
- The one step never to move.

Do not call a step routine without saying why.
