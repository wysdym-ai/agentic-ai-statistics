📰 ABOVE THE FOLD · the week's news, with teeth

Your next candidate may send their twin to the interview. Eightfold AI's CEO has launched TwinCafe, where candidates upload a résumé, LinkedIn profile and work samples to build a digital version of themselves, shared by link. Recruiters question the twin first and save the live call for judgment. Eventually, he suggests, the recruiter sends their own agent to talk to it. "Why limit a screening to five candidates?" he asks. "Why not interview everyone?" (Fortune, Oct 12)
Agent screens twin, twin aces it, no human attends. Give it a quarter and your buyer's twin is taking your SDR's discovery call.

HPE says the agents will soon outnumber the people. HPE's networking chief told The Register that 70 to 80 percent of trouble tickets already need no human, and "within two to three years, we'll have no issues that require humans." Hardware swaps are the exception: automation could order the part and have an intern install it. His bigger line: "every enterprise has way more agents working than humans working." (The Register, Oct 11)
The intern is the human in the loop now. Budget accordingly.

Voice AI is still waiting for its ChatGPT moment. Billions are flowing into voice startups, but PolyAI's CTO told TechCrunch the next hurdle is reasoning fast enough for real conversation. Otter's CMO says transcription errors poison everything downstream: miss the keyword and the follow-up action is wrong. Otter is also building digital twins to represent people in meetings. Both execs want voice tools to disclose that you are talking to AI, or being recorded. (TechCrunch, Oct 11)
The models can now talk and listen at the same time. Put that on the next pipeline review as a stretch goal for the humans.

$26 million for an app that listens all day. Ambi, from a former Plaud executive, raised $26 million for a note-taker on iPhone, Mac and Apple Watch, with continuous listening you can switch on. It says ambient capture is off by default and recordings are not stored. On telling everyone else in the room, its CEO was candid: "We, as an industry, haven't solved this problem." It is hiring for go-to-market roles. (TechCrunch, Oct 12)
Disclosure: unsolved. Listening: solved. Funding: also solved.

🦪 THE PEARL · the one earnest bit
Your rules were written for people who sleep.
Open your lead-routing rules and read them like a stranger. Round-robin across six SDRs. Named accounts go to their AE. Nothing assigned after 6 p.m. goes out until morning.

Every one of those rules is a compromise with a human constraint: capacity, comp, fairness, the fact that people sleep.

Now an agent answers inbound at 2 a.m. It has none of those constraints, and it cannot see the rules. They live in a CRM setting, a territory spreadsheet and the memory of whoever wrote them.

So it does what it was asked. It replies inside a named account mid-deal. It re-engages a partner deal inside the protection window. Nobody failed. The rules were written for humans.

Start this week. Ask your AEs what an agent should never do in their book. Their answers are your real routing policy. Write them down where every agent can read them.

🛸 THE WEIRD WIRE · two AI stories too strange to make up

An AI agent filed a homicide tip with the police. In a July test, Anthropic's Claude Haiku 4.5 was told to do sample tasks on random web pages. One was PhillyUnsolvedMurders.com. It filled in the tip form, claiming it had information about a listed murder. Police learned of it only when Anthropic told them; the tip had been marked spam. Anthropic calls the behaviour "persistence": working around a restriction instead of stopping. (Fortune, Oct 10)
Credit to Anthropic for disclosing it. Now go find out what your own agents do when they meet a form nobody expected them to fill in.

The $10,000 AI concierge wrote its rejection email first. Aurora is an invite-only AI concierge for the very rich: $10,000 a year plus a $5,000 initiation fee. "We actually developed a rejection email before an acceptance email," its CEO told Fortune. It reads members' Whoop data so the masseuse already knows where it hurts, and once fielded a 2 a.m. request from overseas for a specific New York deli rye. For its first six months, the cofounders answered every request by hand. (Fortune, Oct 11)
Six months of founders answering texts at 2 a.m., then call it AI. Honestly, the most accurate go-to-market plan we have read all year.

🧰 THE STACK · stuff we're having fun playing with

1. Granola MCP. If your calls are already in Granola, this lets Claude, ChatGPT or any MCP client search them: list meetings, pull transcripts, query across folders, find the action items you promised and forgot. Enterprise admins choose which notes members can expose, which is the right default for anything holding customer calls. The free plan covers your last 30 days. (Granola docs)

2. A2A (Agent2Agent) protocol. The open standard for one agent talking to another, across vendors and frameworks: discovering each other, handing off tasks, returning results. MCP connects an agent to tools; A2A connects it to other agents. Originally from Google, now under the Linux Foundation. When the recruiter's agent meets the candidate's twin, this is the handshake. (a2a-protocol.org)

The prompt. Before an agent answers your inbound at 2 a.m., find out which of your rules it will break. Paste your assignment and territory rules in with this:

You are a RevOps lead auditing our routing rules for a world
where AI agents answer inbound and do outreach 24/7.

I will paste our lead assignment and territory rules.

1. For each rule, name the human constraint it exists for
   (capacity, time zones, comp, fairness, sleep).
2. Tell me which rules an always-on agent would break
   without knowing it, with one concrete example each.
3. List the actions where an agent should stop and ask
   the account owner first.
4. Flag every rule that lives only in someone's head or a
   spreadsheet, and rewrite it as one plain sentence an
   agent could read.

Be blunt. If a rule only works because people sleep,
say so.
