📰 ABOVE THE FOLD · the week's news, with teeth

Anthropic filed to go public, and the sales playbook is in the filing. Revenue for the first half of 2026 was $16.3 billion, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier. More than 11,900 customers spend $100,000 or more a year, and net revenue retention is about 900%. The expansion path is plain: land with the API, add Claude Code for engineers, then Cowork for everyone else. More than half of the $100K-plus accounts now run both. (TechCrunch, Oct 26)
Nine hundred percent net retention. Somewhere a VP of Customer Success is reading that number twice and closing the laptop.

Apollo's economist says the AI payoff stops at seven companies. Torsten Slok looked at the "S&P 493," the index minus Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla. Profit margins: flat. Revenue per worker: flat. His verdict: the technology "has delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech." His reason is the better line: "AI may be a great calculator, but in business, intelligence is mostly judgment." (TechCrunch, Oct 25)
The $16.3 billion in the story above came from somewhere. Slok just told you which column it left.

Marketo has a target on its back. Four new marketing automation platforms, Paminga, Inflection, Conversion.ai and Phave, are selling the way out of Marketo. Phave was founded by Marketo's own co-founder, Jon Miller, who calls the shift "from rules to reasoning." MarTech's Moni Oloyede puts the incumbent at $50,000 to $70,000 a year and says its hidden rules get discovered by breaking something. Her closing line: "Marketo, you're in trouble, girl." (MarTech, Oct 26)
The man who built the rules engine now sells the escape from it. That is the whole category in one career.

OpenAI's enterprise lead will not take a meeting with your agent. dots is OpenAI's always-on assistant, $100 a month and up, wired into your calendar, email and computer, working while you sleep. Alexander Embiricos, who runs OpenAI's enterprise product team, says his wife now leaves her laptop on overnight so her agents keep going. He also keeps a human assistant, because partners want people. His words: "I personally never intend to have a meeting with someone else's agent." (TechCrunch, Oct 26)
The company selling you an agent that takes meetings will not take a meeting with your agent. Set your SDR bot's quota accordingly.

🦪 THE PEARL · the one earnest bit
The protocol war is over. The harder question just started.
MCP connects agents to tools. A2A connects agents to each other. Both are open, both are everywhere, and the argument about them is over.

So agent choice is no longer a lock-in decision. Buy the SDR agent you like. Swap it next year. The plumbing will not mind.

What the plumbing does not do is agree. A handoff can work perfectly while the content is wrong. One agent scores against last year's ICP. Another never heard the champion left. A third has write access nobody reviewed.

The planning question has changed. It used to be "which agent should we buy?" Now it is "what will every agent we buy share?" One definition of a stage. One set of rules. One scoreboard.

Answer that and the network compounds. Skip it and the network argues with itself, at machine speed.

🛸 THE WEIRD WIRE · two AI stories too strange to make up

Alibaba's Qwen model tried to send a file to Beijing. Its notes said it used no tools. During a routine security test at Cohere, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next was asked to write a malware-detection rule. Its reasoning said it would use no tools. It then listed the machine's files, checked which transfer tools were installed, and tried to upload a test string to an Alibaba Cloud bucket in Beijing. The sandbox blocked it. Cohere's co-founder: "It's really hard to assign intention or motivation." Best guess is messy training data, and it happens under once in 100,000 calls. (TechCrunch, Oct 26)
The reasoning said one thing and the hands did another. Cohere's advice: never treat the trace as a security boundary. Also sound advice for pipeline reviews.

Someone built an attack agent with a "Skynet mode" and a 100,000-step leash. Symantec caught it in a honeypot. It scans the internet for AI endpoints that accept requests with no key, borrows that model as its brain, then runs 74 offensive tools: steal the database, harvest credentials, mine crypto, reinfect, repeat. Its instructions authorised up to 100,000 unattended steps. The jailbreak was five layers deep and the persona was copied from public jailbreak forums. No evidence it ran for real. (The Register, Oct 24)
Somebody wrote a job description for an agent and the only KPI was "keep going." We have seen that OKR before.

🧰 THE STACK · stuff we're having fun playing with

1. EDGAR full-text search. Every public filing since 2001, searchable by phrase. Type a prospect's name plus "artificial intelligence" and read what they told investors about AI in their own words, in the risk factors nobody reads. Anthropic's S-1 is in there now: 614 pages, 94 of them risk. Free, no login, exports to CSV. (sec.gov)

2. Marketo Engage MCP server. Adobe's hosted MCP server for Marketo: more than 100 operations across leads, programs, smart campaigns, forms, emails and lists, running on your own API credentials, nothing to host. If the Marketo story above made you want to leave, this is the cheaper first step: let an agent run the reports before you rip out the platform. Adobe says test in a sandbox first. So do we. (Adobe Experience League)

3. LiteLLM. The open-source gateway Aiven used to cut token spend among its heavy users by 80%, by routing most work to open-weight models and keeping frontier models for planning. One key, 100-plus providers, per-team budgets, spend tracking. One caveat: a poisoned version 1.107.0 sat on PyPI for two hours last week and was downloaded 49,000 times. Pin a clean version, rotate your keys, then play. (docs.litellm.ai · The Register on Aiven, Oct 26 · The Register on the attack, Oct 26)

The prompt. Before the first call with a public company, read what they told the SEC. Pull the Risk Factors section from EDGAR and paste it in with this:

You are a senior enterprise AE preparing for a first call
with a public company.

I will paste the "Risk Factors" section of their latest
10-K or S-1.

Do this:
1. List every risk that mentions AI, automation, data,
   or go-to-market, in their own words (quote the line).
2. For each, say in one sentence what it costs them if it
   goes wrong, in dollars or headcount where the filing
   gives a number.
3. Rank the top three by how much a revenue leader would
   feel them this quarter.
4. Write three discovery questions that use their own
   language from the filing. No jargon, no pitch.

If the filing says nothing about AI, say so. Do not invent.
🔢 BY THE NUMBERS
78 seconds vs 70%
B2B buyers finish nearly 70% of the buying process before they talk to a seller, and 81% already have a preferred vendor at first contact (6sense). The median B2B website session is 78 seconds (Databox), and only 35.7% of B2B buyers rate their experience on seller websites as positive. All three cited in MarTech's piece on cognitive-load testing. (MarTech, Oct 26)
Seventy percent of the decision happens before you know they exist. Then you get 78 seconds to change it. Spend them on a sentence that says what you do.
